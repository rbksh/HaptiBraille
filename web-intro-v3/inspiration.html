Chapter 5 of 8: Inspiration

What inspired us

Two hundred years of people reading by touch and by rhythm, and a motor in every pocket.

HaptiBraille isn't a brand-new idea so much as three old ideas meeting a device in everyone's pocket. These are the moments that shaped it.

  1. 1821

    Reading in the dark: Barbier's night writing

    Charles Barbier, a French army captain, designed "night writing": a code of raised dots that soldiers could read by touch, without lighting a lamp that would give away their position. He showed it at the Royal Institute for Blind Youth in Paris. It used up to twelve dots per symbol, which was too large to feel with one fingertip.

  2. 1824

    A fifteen-year-old makes it fit a fingertip

    Louis Braille, a blind student at that school, simplified Barbier's idea into a cell of just six dots, small enough to feel in one touch. He published it in 1829. Two hundred years later it is still how blind people read worldwide. The lesson for us: a small, consistent pattern is easier to learn than a big one.

  3. 1838

    Language made of timing: Morse code

    Samuel Morse and Alfred Vail's code turned letters into short and long signals separated by pauses. Telegraph operators learned to read it fluently by ear, purely from rhythm. That proved something we rely on: people can read letters from timing alone, if the rhythm is clear and consistent.

  4. 1960s

    A knuckle and a wall: the tap code

    Prisoners of war held in isolation communicated by tapping letters on cell walls, a few taps for the row and a few for the column. With no shared language beyond knocks, they held real conversations. Touch and rhythm were enough.

  5. 2017

    Phones gain precise vibration control

    Android 8 added waveform vibrations: an app can now play an exact pattern of taps and silences, timed to the millisecond, with different strengths. Phones in billions of pockets became capable of playing a precise rhythm.

  6. Today

    HaptiBraille puts them together

    Braille's six-dot cell, Morse's idea of reading by timing, and the tap code's proof that touch is enough, played on the vibration motor people already carry. Add a training course for the 9 in 10 who haven't learned yet, and that's HaptiBraille.
